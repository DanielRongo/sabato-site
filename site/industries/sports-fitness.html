Why this category phones

Most of the catalogue never generates a call. The heavy end generates nothing but.

Sports retail is really two businesses sharing a phone number. Apparel and consumables behave like fashion — the calls arrive after the parcel, about size exchanges and late deliveries. Heavy equipment behaves like furniture: nobody spends four figures on a treadmill without asking about the doorway and the delivery.

Treating them as one queue is why the line feels unmanageable. They need different answers from different systems, which is exactly what a workflow split is for.

Five questions a sports line hears every day — and what has to be true in your systems to answer them

The first two come from the apparel half of the catalogue and the last three from the equipment half. Same line, different data behind them.

“Where is my order?”

Which half of the catalogue it came from decides the answer: a parcel carrier event, or a two-person delivery slot that has to be rebooked.

product.delivery_type · carrier.events

“These trainers come up narrow — can I swap the size?”

Fitting width on the model as well as the size, plus live availability in the wide option — narrow is often a fitting problem, not a size one.

variant.fitting_width · returns.window

“Will the rower fit through a standard doorway?”

Packed dimensions and weight, plus whether it folds — the three facts that decide a heavy-equipment order.

product.packed_dims · attr.folds

“Do you bring it to the room, or leave it at the door?”

Delivery service level for that product: kerbside, threshold, or room of choice with assembly.

product.delivery_type · service.assembly

“The console on my treadmill has failed — is it covered?”

Warranty term by component and the parts availability for that model year.

attr.warranty_by_part · spares.by_model

What the call sounds like

Inbound · 2 min 04 s · language: English · outcome: room-of-choice delivery booked

CALLER

I’m looking at the rower, but it’s going in a spare room upstairs and I want to know if it’ll get up there.

AGENT

Packed, it’s 218 by 56 by 40 centimetres and 44 kilos, in one box. That goes through a standard doorway, but 44 kilos up a flight of stairs is a two-person job.

CALLER

That’s what I was worried about.

AGENT

Then take the room-of-choice delivery rather than the standard one. Two people, they carry it to the room you want and take the packaging away. It’s twenty-nine pounds on this item.

CALLER

And does it need assembling?

AGENT

Minimal — the seat rail and the console, about twenty minutes. Assembly on the same visit is another twenty pounds if you’d rather not.

CALLER

Do the delivery and the assembly.

AGENT

Both added. Next available in your postcode is Thursday week, with a two-hour window texted the night before.

What the agent has to read

The data reality behind a sports answer

The split catalogue is the thing to get right. If equipment and apparel are treated as one product type, the agent will offer kerbside delivery on a treadmill and a two-person slot on a t-shirt.

THE HONEST PART

Bad source data produces confident wrong answers. If a field is stale, the agent will speak it fluently — and a wrong answer at speed is worse than a slow right one, because it converts into an order that fails. We audit the fields this page depends on before the pilot, and we cap the agent to hand over on anything unmapped.

Carrier tracking events

For the apparel half, where volume is highest and complexity lowest.

Returns window and live sizes

So a size problem becomes an exchange rather than a refund.

Packed dimensions and weight

For equipment. Weight matters as much as size once stairs are involved.

Delivery service level per product

Kerbside, threshold or room-of-choice — set on the product, not assumed.

Warranty by component and spares

Equipment fails by part, and the warranty usually differs by part.

Common questions

Our catalogue is mostly clothing — is this worth it?

Then your case is the post-purchase queue: deliveries, exchanges, refunds. It is the same argument as fashion. If that queue is large or spikes hard around sale weeks, there is cost to remove; if it is small and steady, we will tell you so.

Can it handle the heavy equipment side properly?

That is what the packed dimensions, weight and delivery-service-level fields are for. It answers the doorway and stairs question, then books the right delivery type — which is where most equipment orders actually stall.

Does it know when to offer an exchange instead of a refund?

Yes, where availability is live. Offering the adjacent size while the customer is on the phone is the single cheapest retention move in this category.

What about sale weeks and product drops?

Concurrency. Every call is answered at once rather than queued, which is when abandoned calls spike — and the abandoned ones skew towards customers with a problem.

What does it need to go live?

Orders and carrier data, live variant availability, and for equipment: packed dimensions, weight and delivery service level per product.
